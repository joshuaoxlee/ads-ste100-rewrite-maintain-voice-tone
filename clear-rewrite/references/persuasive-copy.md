# Persuasive copy

Use this guidance for marketing, sales, fundraising, advocacy, proposals, landing pages, advertisements, and similar writing. Keep the voice and meaning protections in SKILL.md. Persuasion is part of the content's purpose, not a style defect to remove.

## Identify the job of the copy

Infer from the brief and source: who is being addressed, what matters to them, what is offered, why they should believe it, and what action the copy asks them to take. Identify the promise, supporting evidence, and any conditions. Keep this analysis internal unless asked.

A clarity edit should preserve the existing level of persuasion. A request to make the copy more compelling permits changes to emphasis, framing, rhythm, and structure. It does not supply new facts or authorize a different offer.

## Strengthen expression

- Bring the central value or hook into focus. A benefit can lead when supported by the source; do not invent an audience problem to make the opening dramatic.
- Connect a feature to a benefit only when the source or supplied brief supports that connection. Otherwise describe the feature clearly or ask for the missing benefit if essential.
- Make evidence easier to find and understand. Preserve who achieved a result, under what conditions, and over what period.
- Keep personality: sensory language, humour, aspiration, metaphor, rhetorical questions, purposeful repetition, and punchy fragments can all work. Remove a phrase because it is vague or ineffective in context, not because it sounds like marketing.
- Make the next step clear and consistent with the offer. "Explore the collection" and "Buy now" ask for different commitments. Do not change the desired action, purchase stage, or destination unless requested or supported by the brief.
- Keep the existing degree of urgency unless the user asks to change it. Any factual deadline or scarcity claim must come from the source or confirmed brief.
- Address an objection only with information available in the source or brief. Do not invent a refund policy, compatibility claim, trial, guarantee, or reassurance.

## Keep claims honest to the source

Do not invent testimonials, customer counts, awards, research, savings, discounts, rankings, performance measurements, or endorsements. Do not turn a subjective impression into a measurable promise, a possibility into a guarantee, or a single customer's experience into a universal result.

Retain material qualifiers close enough to the claim that the reader can understand the offer. Do not move an eligibility rule into a remote note merely to make the headline stronger. Preserve currencies, billing intervals, renewal terms, exclusions, and other conditions provided in the source.

Distinguish evocative language from factual claims. "A little holiday in every sip" can remain a metaphor. "Cuts anxiety by 50%" is a measurable claim; do not create it from a general statement about feeling relaxed.

Rewriting does not establish that an existing claim is true. Preserve supplied claims at their existing strength without implying that you verified them. If there is a clear contradiction or the user requests an unsupported new claim, flag the specific issue briefly and offer wording grounded in what is known. Do not interrupt routine editing to demand evidence for every adjective.

## Adapt to the channel

Keep channel conventions when they help: subject lines for an email, headline and button text for a landing page, spoken rhythm for a pitch, or a compact hook for a social post. Do not impose a funnel formula or add sections, hashtags, emojis, or a new call to action by default. When adapting to another channel, preserve material terms and carry over the intended action unless the brief changes it.

If the user requests alternatives, vary a meaningful dimension such as the hook, emphasis, or tone while holding facts and terms constant. Do not manufacture stronger results just to make one version stand out.
